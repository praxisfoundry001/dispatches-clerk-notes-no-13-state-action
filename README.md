# Dispatches from the Record: Clerk Notes No. 13

**State Action: When Private-Looking Conduct Carries Public Authority**

## What is this?

This is Clerk Notes No. 13 in the Dispatches from the Record series.

It offers a plain-language structural reading of State Action: the doctrine that asks when private-looking conduct is attributable to public authority strongly enough for constitutional responsibility to attach.

## Why should I care?

State Action can sound like technical gatekeeping.

But the human problem underneath it is simple and difficult: a person may be harmed by a private actor, and that harm may be real, serious, coercive, or unlawful under some other body of law. Constitutional responsibility usually requires something more.

The question is not only whether harm happened.

The question is whether public authority moved through the act.

## What is the claim?

The core claim is:

**State Action asks when the civic body owns the act.**

Private harm may be real without becoming constitutional harm. But public power cannot escape constitutional accountability by moving through private form.

State Action protects private space while preventing public responsibility from laundering itself through private-looking conduct.

## What is this not?

This is not legal advice.

It does not replace State Action doctrine, civil-rights litigation, § 1983 analysis, public-function doctrine, joint-action doctrine, state-compulsion doctrine, entwinement analysis, official-capacity analysis, or any other technical legal test.

It does not claim every private harm is constitutional harm. It does not claim private-looking actors can never become constitutionally accountable. It does not decide hard State Action cases.

## How was this done?

The note translates State Action through the Dispatches method: doctrine first, plain language, structural compression, Constitutional Physiology where useful, and failure-signature diagnostics.

It treats State Action as a discipline of attribution. It asks who acted, in what capacity, with whose authority, through what pathway, who supplied the power, who ratified the result, and who owns the consequence.
